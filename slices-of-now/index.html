Flatland, spacetime and the fourth dimension

Slices of Now

A Flatlander lives on a plane and can only see what lies in it. Push a ball through their world and they watch a circle appear from nowhere, grow, shrink and vanish. Many physicists describe our lives the same way, one dimension up: all of history is a four-dimensional block, and each moment you live through is a three-dimensional slice of it.

The pink sheet below is Flatland. Grab the shapes and push them through it, then follow the idea up a dimension.

2D world + 3D visitors

A Sphere visits Flatland

In Edwin Abbott's 1884 novella Flatland, the narrator is A Square, who lives in a world with length and breadth but no height. A solid object only exists for him where it crosses his plane. The panel beside the 3D view shows exactly that: the cross-section of every shape, seen from above. The strip under it is closer to what A Square really sees, a single line of color.

“You call me a Circle; but in reality I am not a Circle, but an infinite number of Circles, of size varying from a Point to a Circle of thirteen inches in diameter, one placed on the top of the other.”

The Sphere, explaining itself to A Square · Flatland, 1884

Pick the ball and press Lower it slowly. A Square sees a point, then a circle that grows, shrinks back to a point and disappears. Do the same with a cube or a pyramid and the slices change shape as it turns: triangles, squares, rectangles, even hexagons. Nothing in A Square's experience tells him these all belong to one rigid object. He meets the slices one after another, so to him a solid looks like a flat shape that changes over time.

  • Drop a torus. Lying flat, it slices into a ring. Standing on its edge, it slices into two separate circles that merge into one oval near the top.
  • Press Scan to sweep Flatland up and down through everything resting on the floor, like a scanner building up a body one slice at a time.
  • Grab a cube by one corner and hold it so the opposite corner points down through the sheet. Its slice starts as a triangle and widens into a hexagon.

2D world + time = 3D block

Flatland's history is a loaf

Now treat time as a direction. Below is a small Flatland where balls slide around and collide. Record every moment, stack the frames with the earliest at the bottom, and you get a 3D solid: a loaf of spacetime. Each ball becomes a tube, called its worldtube. A collision is a kink where two tubes touch. A ball sitting still would be a straight vertical column.

Nothing in the loaf moves. What a Flatlander experiences as motion is the "now" slice rising through it. Drag the Now slider and the panel on the right shows that slice and nothing else. The past is drawn solid and the future as glass, but in this picture both are equally there. Physicists call this view the block universe.

Clicking inside Flatland adds a ball at the current moment and recalculates everything above the slice. Inside the block picture nobody can do that. The future is already part of the shape, just like the past. (South pull is a nod to the book, where everything in Flatland feels a slight attraction toward the South.)

Now tilt the slice. Special relativity says that observers moving relative to each other disagree about which distant events happen at the same moment. In this picture, each observer cuts the same loaf at a different angle, so a tilted "now" catches each ball at a different time. The panel labels each ball with the moment it was caught. Neither cut is the true one, and that is one of the main reasons physicists take the whole block seriously. A real spacetime diagram tilts by the rules of Minkowski geometry rather than ordinary angles, but the idea is the same.

3D world + 4D visitors

A hypersphere visits us

Go up one dimension. We are the Flatlanders now, and our whole space is the sheet. Call the extra direction w. A 4D ball, or hypersphere, drifting through our space along w would look the way the sphere looked to A Square: a point appears out of nothing, swells into a ball, shrinks and vanishes.

Four-dimensional polytopes do stranger things. A tesseract (a 4D cube) passing straight through shows up as a cube that sits still for a while and then disappears. Turn it in 4D first so it arrives corner first, and you see a tiny tetrahedron that grows, has its corners cut off, becomes an octahedron, and then reverses. All of those are slices of one rigid object.

Now pick Thrown ball's worldtube. This 4D shape is a curved tube: the history of one ordinary ball, with w standing in for time. Every slice of it is a single ball, and sliding w makes that ball fly and bounce. The tube itself never moves or changes. All of the motion comes from which slice you are looking at.

Show 4D shadow flattens the whole object into our space, the way a cube casts a flat shadow on a wall. The shadow is a picture of the entire 4D object. The solid slice is the only part of it that actually exists here.

3D world + time = 4D block

You are a slice too

Put the pieces together. Flatland plus time made a 3D loaf, which we could draw. Our space plus time makes a 4D block that nobody can draw, but it follows the same rules. Your body right now is a 3D cross-section of a long 4D worldtube that starts before you were born and runs to the end of your life. "Now" is where the slice happens to be.

Where the picture breaks

  • Time is not quite a fourth direction of space. In relativity, the separation between two events subtracts the time part instead of adding it (the Minkowski metric). That minus sign is why nothing can outrun light, and why tilting a slice follows hyperbolic rules instead of the ordinary rotation drawn in plate II.
  • The block does not say why the slice moves forward. The basic laws of motion work the same forwards and backwards. The arrow of time is usually traced to entropy, which was very low at the Big Bang and has been rising ever since. Memory and cause-and-effect run in the direction entropy increases.
  • It is one interpretation. The block universe, also called eternalism, is popular with physicists because of the relativity of simultaneity. Presentism (only now is real) and the growing block (the past and present are real, the future is not yet) both still have serious defenders, and quantum mechanics adds open questions of its own.

When A Square tried to tell the other Flatlanders about the third dimension, he kept repeating "Upward, not Northward," and was jailed for heresy. The extra direction on this page is easier to find. You travel along it every second.
